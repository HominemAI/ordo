Inter-space sharing: the consent surface, and three designs

Superseded for the build, 2026-09-29: the plan is docs/plan-sharing.md (guests, the window as default, and share links). This page stays the reference for the 22 consent points and the three designs.

Guest windows and consented feeds are settled in the language and specified in the conformance corpus. Neither exists in code. Before either gets built, the question that actually decides the architecture is where a human says yes. That is itemized first, and the three designs are three different answers to it.

Status: options for a decision. Nothing here is settled.  Sources read: definition-language.md §3 (members, acl, sealed), §7 (triggers), §8 (access model, inter-space sharing, bootstrap, exit ops); guards-conformance.md R-G11a, R-G12, R-G13, R-G18, R-G20, R-G21, GC059–GC066, GC092; audit-board-coverage.md findings 2–3; bootstrap.md §5, §8; deploying.md §5; implementation.md (outbound egress, client delivery, mirrors); security-logging-design.md §5; glossary.md (feed vs window); crates/ordo-identity/src/{pins,grants}.rs; crates/ordo-conformance/tests/guards_corpus.rs.

0 · Where this starts from

definition-language.md §8 settles two doors and calls them complementary: window vs copy:

DoorWhat it isWhere the data livesRevocation
Window (guest access)An acl row naming an outside principal; the guest calls the granting space's guard-bearing ops under their own identity, attributed in that space's chain Only in the sharer's spaceCloses at the next call
Copy (consented feed)A standing routine replicating a named query's output one-way into the receiving space, landing as ordinary writes attributed source:feed-<name> In both spaces, independently ownedStops future copies; already-copied data stays

What is already true in the tree, and it matters for the recommendation: the window's runtime semantics are conformance-tested and passing. GC059, GC060, GC061, GC062 and GC066 are in guards_corpus.rs's EXECUTED list: a guest reaching exactly their ACL row's guard, no role fallback, write refused at read level, and the window closing on revoke. The corpus does that by making the guest an ordinary members row with a null role and one grant. What is missing is the registration and the consent record: GC063, GC064 and GC065 sit in NOT_RUN with the reason stated outright: "share/exposure registration does not exist: there is no share to guest:… declaration and no guest principal kind."

So this is not a missing enforcement engine. It is a missing consent surface with an enforcement engine already behind it. That framing is why the itemization comes first.

1 · The consent surface, itemized

Twenty-two points where a human says yes, or is owed a disclosure. Each names who, to what (disclosed and pinned), revocation, and what revocation takes effect on. Items in amber are spec-silent. The settled documents do not answer them, and the three options answer them differently.

A · Guest window: sharer side

A1Registering the exposure: which ops a guest may call
Who consents
Someone in the granting space with authority over exposure. Which authority is not stated. DL §8's access model gives the space owner acl administration at the door and nothing else names exposure.
To exactly what
A named list of ops. What is disclosed is each op's manifest: the field-grain read set, the written set, and the row-scope shape. This is the same pair pins.rs already carries for a capability grant (EntityPin{read, written, structure, read_guard, write_guard, caller_restricted}).
What is pinned
Per R-G18 and GC065, the registration records the guard-bearing basis per op: entity guard, inherited acl guard, or $caller condition. An op with no reachable guard and no $caller condition is refused at registration with guest_exposure_unguarded; so is an unguarded-marked op (GC064) and one over an access read open entity (R-G21, GC099/GC100).
Revocable
Yes, by deregistering the exposure.
Takes effect on
Future calls. Nothing already returned is retracted.

DL §8 Inter-space sharing; guards-conformance R-G18, GC063–GC065.

A2The ACL row naming the guest: which rows
Who consents
The holder of the top level on the acl root row, or the space owner via the door-checked acl-administration capability (DL §8 layer 1; R-G13).
To exactly what
The rows that one acl row's guard admits, at that level. Level ordering is total: read ⊂ write ⊂ admin (R-G14). Nothing else in the space becomes visible. GC059 is exactly this assertion, and GC060 is its complement (no role fallback; guards over entities where the guest holds nothing return empty, never an error and never a default view).
What is pinned
Nothing, today. The acl row is live mutable data; widening it later is an ordinary write, not a re-consent event.
Revocable
Yes. It is revokeBoardsAccess-shaped, an addressed delete by unique(root, member) (R-G17).
Takes effect on
The next call (GC066: revoke, then the guest's next boardList returns 0 rows). It stops access, not memory. DL §8 states this without euphemism. implementation.md adds a control §8 does not mention: a revoked space is marked poisoned at next contact and the client purges before serving any read, best-effort against an offline device.

DL §8; guards-conformance R-G14, R-G17, GC059, GC060, GC066; implementation.md client delivery.

A3The custody acknowledgement: is this window even possible
Who consents
The sharer, at share time.
To exactly what
DL §8: "shareability is computed from custody and shown at share time". A window requires the granting space's key-release path, because the guest's read executes in the sharer's space and any sealed field in the op's manifest must be opened there. User-keyed and client-first content is therefore unsharable by window. The platform offers a copy instead.
What is pinned
Which sealed fields the exposed ops' manifests contain. Key release is derived from op access (DL §7): a caller allowed to invoke an op whose manifest includes sealed fields thereby, and only thereby, triggers release for exactly those fields, logged with principal, op, fields, space and instant.
Revocable
By revoking A1 or A2. There is no separate key-release grant to revoke.
Takes effect on
The next call; releases already logged stand as history.

DL §8 (shareability), §7 (key release), §3 (sealed fields, std sealed).

A4Accepting the guest into the space's own audit and roster
Who consents
The sharer, implicitly, by doing A1+A2, but it is a disclosure the sharer's other members receive and do not give.
To exactly what
The guest's calls land in the sharer's command log attributed to the guest as themselves; callersOf answers with them; and the guest's displayName is plaintext skeleton visible to the space (DL §3 pins it as not sealed, because bylines and sort displayName have to work in SQL).
What is pinned
Nothing.
Revocable
The membership row can be suspended or removed; the chain entries cannot.
Takes effect on
Future calls only. The chain is append-only.

DL §8 (attribution), §3 (members, displayName).

B · Guest window: receiver (guest) side

B1Accepting the invitation: principal binding
Who consents
The guest, personally.
To exactly what
Their deployment principal is bound into a space they do not own; the platform mints them a members row with a null role, as an ordinary attributed command in that space's chain (DL §3, minting path iv). DL §8 pins the flow as invitation, never enumeration: link/invite → accept → principal binding; ids are exchanged only on accept; there is no cross-space principal lookup.
What is pinned
Should be the ops' manifests, by symmetry with a capability grant. Nothing says it is.
Revocable
Not stated. See C3.
Takes effect on
Not applicable.

DL §8 (invitation, never enumeration); DL §3 (who creates the members row).

B2Disclosing their own name across the boundary
Who consents
The guest.
To exactly what
DL §3 is unusually precise here: "guest binding takes the name the guest consented to disclose at accept time, which is the only cross-space disclosure and is therefore a consent decision, not a lookup." This is the single piece of the guest's own identity that crosses.
What is pinned
The disclosed string. Later renames propagate as ordinary attributed platform commands in every space the principal belongs to.
Revocable
Only by leaving (see C3).
Takes effect on
Not applicable.

DL §3 (displayName and where it comes from).

B3The owner-reach disclosure
Who consents
The guest. They are joining a space they do not own, so bootstrap.md §8's obligation applies to them at full strength.
To exactly what
The sentence the corpus asks be said in the invitee's own language, not a policy link: "the owner of this space can reach anything in it, and every time they do it is logged." Z-004 is settled the way that makes this a disclosure and not a fix: no acl row is private from the space owner, because an owner who can administer acl rows can grant themselves any level on any row. The corollary belongs beside it: members who need rows the owner cannot reach need a different space, not a different rule.
What is pinned
Nothing. It is a disclosure, not a grant.
Revocable
n/a.
Takes effect on
n/a.

DL §8 access model (Z-004); bootstrap.md §8 obligation 2; deploying.md §5.

B4 · silentThe guest's device now mirrors someone else's space
Who should consent
The guest.
To exactly what
implementation.md pins mirrors as per-space, separately keyed, sealed at rest under a device key, auto-locking, with policy mirror { keep 90d | keep <n> rows | full } surfaced on the space's consent page, because "a mirror is a copy, and copies are consent-relevant." A guest's device holds such a mirror of the sharer's space. DL §8 never says whose consent page carries that policy, or that the guest sees it at all.
Why it matters
It is the concrete mechanism behind "revocation stops access, not memory". The mirror-scope dial is the control that actually bounds how much memory there is.

Derived: implementation.md (client delivery, "the local store is a database"); DL §8 is silent.

B5 · silentThe guest's app needs its own grant in the sharer's space
Who should consent
The guest, again, on a second consent page.
To exactly what
security-logging-design.md §5 pins "a grant is scoped to exactly one space" and "a grant spanning two spaces is two grants, consented twice." So a guest who reaches the sharer's space through any client other than a raw human session needs a capability grant in the sharer's space, with its own op allowlist, its own per-op pins and its own mandatory expiry. Nothing in §8 mentions this second consent, and a user experiencing it will meet two prompts for what feels like one act.
Why it matters
It is where the existing consent machinery already lives, and option A below proposes making it the only consent record rather than a second one.

Derived: security-logging-design.md §5; ordo-identity/src/{grants,pins}.rs. DL §8 is silent.

C · Consented feed: sharer side

C1Consenting to the slice
Who consents
The sharing space's owner or manager. DL §8 says only "both ends consent".
To exactly what
A named query's output, chosen deliberately so the shared surface has a manifest. That is the whole disclosure: the query's output shape and its row scope, applied not to today's rows but to every row it will ever admit, for as long as the feed stands.
What is pinned
Should be the named query's manifest hash and row-scope shape. Not stated.
Revocable
Yes.
Takes effect on
Future copies only. Everything already sent stays, and stays the receiver's. DL requires this be stated at consent time, not discovered at revocation time.

DL §8 Consented feeds; glossary.md "feed vs window".

C2 · silentKey release for sealed fields in the slice
Who should consent
The sharer.
To exactly what
Custody keys are per space. The receiver cannot decrypt the sharer's sealed fields, ever. So a feed carrying a sealed field is not a byte transfer: it is unseal-in-S, transport, re-seal-under-R's-key, repeated per batch, for the life of the feed. Each unseal is a key release under DL §7's derived rule and must be logged. §8 says nothing about sealed data in feeds; §7's release rule is written for op access by a caller, and a standing routine is not a caller in that sense.
Why it matters
This is the single largest hidden cost in the feed design, and the option that ignores it ships a plaintext hop nobody consented to.

Derived: DL §3 (sealed), §7 (key release), §8 (feeds). The composition is stated nowhere.

C3 · silentThe egress destination, when the receiver is elsewhere
Who should consent
The sharing space's owner.
To exactly what
implementation.md is pinned that feed delivery uses the same egress path as every other outbound fetcher: https-only, resolve-then-pin with connect-to-resolved-address, no redirects, signed deliveries, a credential-less egress identity with no KeyProvider reachability, and a per-space egress allowlist consented at sink creation and rendered on the space's consent page: "adding a host is a consent event, never a config edit." DL §8 does not mention egress at all, and says nothing about whether a same-deployment feed skips it.
Why it matters
If a same-deployment feed bypasses the allowlist, the consent page for a feed says less than the consent page for a webhook that carries the same rows.

implementation.md "Outbound egress: sinks, webhooks & feeds [pinned]"; security-logging-design.md §7b. DL §8 is silent.

D · Consented feed: receiver side

D1Accepting the feed at all
Who consents
The receiving space's owner or manager. DL's reason is one line and it is the right one: "a feed nobody asked for is spam."
To exactly what
Ongoing writes into their own space, attributed source:feed-<name>, which they will own outright. One-way in v1, with no write-back; DL calls bidirectional sync a different product.
What is pinned
Not stated. By symmetry with C1 it should be the same manifest.
Revocable
Not stated from this side. §8 says "revocation stops the feed" and names no revoker. See E3.
Takes effect on
Future copies.

DL §8 Consented feeds.

D2Accepting that inbound data drives local automation
Who consents
The receiver. This is the consequence with teeth.
To exactly what
Copies land as ordinary writes on the receiver's chain, so the receiver's own triggers and rules fire on them. DL calls this out as the point: cross-space reactivity becomes local reactivity, which is what makes feeds the blessed app-to-app interface. §7 gives it a dedicated event kind, fed ("a write attributed to source:feed-*"), and notes that a listener on written sees feed copies too.
What is pinned
The bound on what a woken routine may touch. R-G20 is the load-bearing rule: a listener-woken routine may read and write only rows reachable from $trigger.ids, precisely because ingested and fed triggers are fired by outsiders and a globally guard-exempt listener is a confused deputy an outsider steers. Ranging wider requires run(…) unguarded, one greppable word.
Revocable
By stopping the feed, or by the receiver changing their own triggers.
Takes effect on
Future arrivals. Automation already run has run.

DL §8; §7 (event kinds, fed); guards-conformance R-G20, GC096–GC098.

D3Taking custody of the copies
Who consents
The receiver.
To exactly what
The copies sit under the receiver's custody position, the receiver's keys, the receiver's jurisdiction, the receiver's retention terms, and the receiver's own owner-reach property (B3, now pointed the other way: the receiver's owner can reach the sharer's data). Revocation does not take them back; export and deletion of them are the receiver's exit ops.
What is pinned
Nothing.
Revocable
Only by the receiver deleting the rows, which is an ordinary write.
Takes effect on
Immediately, in the receiver's space.

DL §8 (receiver owns the copies outright); §8 Exit ops; deploying.md §5.

D4Both consents land in both chains
Who consents
Both, once each.
To exactly what
DL §8's invitation pin says "consent lands in both spaces' chains." Worth stating plainly what that is and is not: the command log is per space and ordering is guaranteed per space only. There is no shared chain. So "both chains" means two independent records that can be reverted, pruned or exported independently, and nothing correlates them unless something is designed to.
What is pinned
Nothing correlating the two.
Revocable
Chain entries never are.
Takes effect on
n/a.

DL §8 (invitation); DL §7 (ordering guaranteed per space only).

E · The silences both doors share, and these are the decisions

E1 · silentDoes changing the shared op kill the share?

For capability grants the answer is settled and implemented: a grant pins the field set and the row-scope shape; narrowing = field-set subset AND row scope no weaker; anything else is expansion and the grant is dead until a human re-consents. pins.rs exists precisely because the field-set-only form was "decidable and wrong": deleting member == $caller from an op body leaves the output field set byte-identical while turning "read my expenses" into "read everyone's expenses". That exact attack applies unchanged to a share and to a feed, and §8 says nothing about it. A share is a consent to a manifest; the manifest can be redeployed underneath it.

E2 · silentDoes a share expire?

Capability grants carry a mandatory expiry: "no unexpiring grants" is pinned, and grants.rs observes that before the registry existed, expiry was a grant's only end. Shares and feeds are specified with no expiry at all, which makes them the one standing authorization in the platform that outlives everything.

E3 · silentCan the receiving side revoke?

Every revocation §8 states is the sharer's: the window closes, the feed stops. A guest who wants out of someone else's space, and a receiver who wants a feed to stop arriving, have no stated verb. "Both ends consent" without "both ends may withdraw" is asymmetric in the direction that favours whoever set it up.

E4 · silentIs exposure syntax or runtime state? The documents disagree.

DL §8's access model opens with a pin: "Who may call what is runtime state, never syntax, never IaC, same boundary as grants." R-G18 and GC063–GC065 put share to guest:gil { openTasks, cardList } in source, as a compile-tier construct with reject: outcomes and guest_exposure_unguarded classified compile in the class inventory. Those cannot both be right as written. Resolution below.

E5 · silentTransitivity and loops, and a rule that is cited but does not exist

May a guest of S, who is an owner of T, re-share S's rows onward? May a feed's output be fed onward, or fed back? DL §7 says a listener on written sees feed copies too, "which is what makes feeds locally reactive, and the anti-loop rule in §8 is what keeps that from being a cycle." There is no anti-loop rule in §8. The nearest thing is "one-way, no write-back in v1", which prevents A→B→A but not A→B→C→A.

E6 · silentWhere is the invitation and consent surface actually specified?

Nowhere. deploying.md §5 says "bootstrap.md §5 asks that anyone joining a space they do not own be told…"; bootstrap.md §8 says "§5's invitation and consent surface must carry the sentence." Both point at bootstrap.md §5, which is titled "What std.space.create must not do" and contains no invitation surface. DL §8 gives the flow one bullet (link/invite → accept → principal binding) and no design. Whichever option is chosen, this is the document that has to be written.

2 · Three designs

Three architectures, not three sizes of one. They differ in where the shared rows live: in the sharer's space (reference), in both (replication), or in neither (relocation).

Option A: Pull-through window, first and alone

ELI2 Your neighbour does not get a copy of your book. You cut a small window in your wall and they can read one shelf through it, standing outside. Board the window up and they stop reading, but they still remember what they already read.

What it makes easy

Easy

  • Revocation that nearly works: next call, plus mirror poisoning.
  • One audit story. The guest's activity is in S's chain, where the data is.
  • No divergence, no second copy to secure, no re-seal pipeline.
  • The enforcement half is already built and tested.

Hard

  • Liveness couples two spaces: if S is down or slow, the guest's app is.
  • Client-first and user-keyed spaces cannot use it at all: there is no key-release path (A3).
  • No app-to-app automation: nothing lands in the guest's chain, so no trigger of theirs can fire.
  • Needs the invitation/consent ceremony that exists in no document (E6).

Forecloses

  • Nothing structurally. DL calls the two doors complementary.
  • But it defers every integration use case until the feed exists.

How it lands on what is already built

  • Guards / EXISTS chains. Nothing changes. A guard is a correlated EXISTS scoped to one space with $caller as a binding slot; the plan is caller-independent (GC057 asserts the version hash is identical across callers). A guest is a different binding, not a different plan.
  • Grant pinning. This option's central move: make the share record a CapabilityGrant whose principal is foreign. grants.rs and pins.rs then supply E1 (manifest expansion kills it), E2 (mandatory expiry), E3 (a revocation record either side can hold), and callersOf's token path, with no new consent object invented.
  • Per-space command log. Untouched and correct: the guest's calls belong in S's log because they happened in S.
  • Per-space custody keys / sealed fields. Sealed fields are readable through the window only where S has a server-side release path. The guest never holds S's keys; S opens the field, for that op, for that run, and logs it. Where S is client-first, DL's own rule applies: refuse the window, offer a copy. That is an argument for building the feed eventually, not first.
  • Triggers. Deliberately inert. No write lands in the guest's space, so no trigger of theirs fires. This is the capability the option gives up.
  • E4 dissolves. Guard-bearing-ness is a property of an op, computable at compile time with no share in sight. Stamp it into the artifact per op; let the binding to a named guest be a runtime grant. DL's "never syntax" pin survives, R-G18's compile check survives, and guest_exposure_unguarded stays a compile class. It fires on the op, not on a share to guest:gil line naming a human in source. GC063/GC064 become assertions about vendorNames and allTasks; GC065 becomes an assertion about the artifact's recorded basis.

Cost: LOW. It is the cheapest of the three, and the gap is largest between what it costs and what it delivers. New: a guest principal kind, a foreign-principal grant path, the per-op guest-exposable mark in the artifact, and the invitation ceremony. It depends on no unbuilt subsystem.

Option B: Consented feed replication, first and alone

ELI2 Nobody looks through a window. Every time your shelf changes, you post your neighbour a photocopy of one page. Those copies are theirs now. They can file them, and their own doorbell rings when one arrives. Stop posting and they keep every copy you already sent.

Easy

  • App-to-app integration, which DL calls the blessed interface.
  • No runtime coupling; R works when S is offline.
  • R's triggers fire, so cross-space reactivity becomes local reactivity.
  • Client-first spaces can share (compute on device, best-effort, device-gated).

Hard

  • Revocation is weak by construction and must be sold as such.
  • Sealed data means a per-batch plaintext hop through S's host, forever (C2).
  • Divergence: two copies, two chains, no correlation (D4).
  • Confused-deputy surface: outsider-attributed writes wake R's automation. R-G20 is the closure and it is unbuilt.

Forecloses

  • Nothing permanently, but it inverts the order: live sharing waits behind three subsystems.
  • It cannot serve "let my accountant look at this, live, and then stop."

How it lands on what is already built

  • Guards. Never evaluated across the boundary. S's guard bounds what the named query returns; after that, R's own guards govern the copies as ordinary rows. This is clean, and it is also the reason revocation cannot reach backwards.
  • Grant pinning. Fits well. The feed is a two-space act, and security-logging-design.md §5 already says "a grant spanning two spaces is two grants, consented twice." A feed is exactly that shape and should be built as literally that: one grant in S (read the slice), one in R (write the copies).
  • Per-space command log. Both sides get a local record and there is no shared one. Feed batches must apply atomically per command group. implementation.md already pins this for the client mirror and the same rule has to hold here.
  • Per-space custody keys / sealed fields. The hardest part, and the one §8 never mentions. R cannot decrypt S's sealed fields; so either the feed carries plaintext-skeleton fields only (safe, and much less useful), or S unseals per batch and R re-seals, which is a standing, logged, indefinite key release granted once. Both are defensible; neither is written down.
  • Triggers. This is the whole point (D2), and it is also the dependency: the fed event kind, debounce/coalesce, at-least-once with dead-lettering, and R-G20's provenance bound must all exist first.
  • Unbuilt substrate it sits on. Routines (no AST node, no parser production; routine is a reserved word only), triggers (operand grammars parse strings, unreachable from parse_program), outbound egress (one comment in ordo-crypto), and the source:feed-* attribution path. That is three "Not started" board rows under one feature.

Cost: HIGH, roughly 3–4× option A, and most of it is not sharing work. Choosing this first is choosing to build the automation tier and the egress subsystem first.

Option C: The linking space: move the boundary instead of crossing it

ELI2 Instead of a window or a photocopy, you and your neighbour build a small shed between your two gardens, and you both keep a key. Anything you want to share, you put in the shed. Nothing ever crosses a wall, because the shared thing was never inside your house.

Easy

  • Zero new authorization surface. Roles, groups, profiles, acl, guards, triggers, chain, export all work unchanged.
  • Revocation is immediate and total: suspend the membership, effective next request.
  • callersOf already answers exhaustively for L.
  • No re-seal pipeline, no egress, no replication lag, no divergence.

Hard

  • The data has to be there. Sharing something that already exists in S still needs a copy, so C alone cannot retrofit.
  • Refs are per space. L's rows cannot point at S's rows, so L is a standalone model, not a view of one.
  • Somebody owns L, and B3's owner-reach disclosure now names them. "A neutral shed" is not a thing the platform can offer.
  • A third custody position, a third jurisdiction, a third retention policy, a third export.

Forecloses

  • Retrofit sharing of existing rows, without one of the other two doors.
  • DL §8 as written. This is a substitution, not a subset: it says the two settled doors are not needed.
  • Fine-grained sharing: the unit becomes a whole space, not an acl subtree.

How it lands on what is already built

  • Guards / EXISTS chains. Unchanged and space-local, which is the entire appeal. Every guard in the corpus already assumes exactly this shape.
  • Grant pinning. Unchanged: an app over L holds an ordinary L-scoped grant, and scope expansion kills it exactly as today.
  • Per-space command log. L has its own, and it is the only shared audit trail any of these three options can produce, because it is one chain both parties can read.
  • Per-space custody keys / sealed fields. L's sealed fields are sealed under L's key, which both members can reach through op access. There is no cross-custody problem, because there is no crossing. The cost is that a row cannot be private-to-S and shared-into-L; it is one or the other, authored once.
  • Triggers. Both parties' automation can fire on L's writes, because both are members of L, and it fires under R-G20's ordinary rules with no outsider-provenance question at all. This is strictly cleaner than the feed's story.
  • What it needs that does not exist. std.space.create as a callable op (the board has it "Blocked: needs a platform-op path"), and the invitation ceremony (E6), which every option needs.

Cost: MEDIUM in engineering, perhaps 1.5× option A, nearly all of it in space provisioning rather than sharing. HIGH in modelling churn: every domain that wants sharing has to be designed for it up front, and modeling-guidance.md §A's space-per-user advice becomes mandatory rather than idiomatic.

4 · Recommendation

Option A, with one amendment: the share record is a capability grant with a foreign principal. Then the feed, second, on top of the trigger tier, as two such grants, one each side. Keep the linking space as documented modelling guidance, and do not build it as machinery.

This is mine to be argued out of. The reasoning, in order of weight:

  • The window is not a build, it is a consent surface over an engine that already passes its tests. Five of the eight guest cases execute against real Postgres today. The three that do not are all registration. Building A first converts a passing-but-unreachable engine into a shipped feature; building B or C first leaves it unreachable for longer.
  • The feed is downstream of three "Not started" board rows. Routines have no AST node. Triggers parse strings unreachable from parse_program. Outbound egress is one comment. Choosing B first is choosing to build the automation tier first while calling it sharing. The automation tier deserves to be sequenced on its own merits, not smuggled in.
  • Making the share a grant closes four of the seven silences for free. E1 (manifest expansion kills it) is the important one, and it is not hypothetical: pins.rs exists because the naive form let someone delete member == $caller and silently turn "read my expenses" into "read everyone's expenses". A share is a longer-lived consent than a grant and is exposed to exactly the same edit. E2 gives it an expiry, E3 gives both sides a revocation record, B5 collapses from two consent prompts to one.
  • It also dissolves E4 rather than ruling on it. Guard-bearing-ness is an op property; compute it at compile time, stamp it in the artifact, and let the binding to a named human be runtime state. R-G18's check survives at full strength and DL's "never syntax, never IaC" pin survives intact. The corpus cases need re-spelling, not re-deciding.
  • C is genuinely attractive and I am not recommending it as machinery, only as guidance. It is the only option with a shared audit trail, and its revocation is the strongest of the three. But it cannot answer the question people actually ask (share this budget with my accountant) without a copy mechanism to populate L, so it does not remove the need for A or B; it adds a third owner with full reach over the shared rows; and refs being per space means L is a separate model rather than a view. It belongs in modeling-guidance.md as the answer to "we want a genuinely shared workspace", which is a different question from "let them see this".

Where to disagree. If the near-term customers are integrations rather than people (app-to-app, machine-to-machine) then B is right and A is the deferral, and the consequence is that sharing waits behind the automation tier. And if the goal this quarter is to add zero new authorization surface, C-as-guidance plus neither door is a defensible answer that costs one paragraph of documentation.

What has to be written either way, because every option needs it: the invitation and consent surface (E6). It is referenced by two documents, exists in neither, and it is where most of the twenty-two items above are actually rendered to a human.

5 · Contradictions found in the settled documents

  1. Exposure registration is syntax and is also forbidden from being syntax. DL §8: "Who may call what is runtime state, never syntax, never IaC." R-G18 + GC063–GC065 put share to guest:gil { openTasks, cardList } in source as a compile-tier construct with guest_exposure_unguarded classified compile. Both are settled. The proposed resolution is in option A: split the op property (compile) from the binding (runtime). Sharpest of the set: it decides where the feature is built.
  2. §7 cites an anti-loop rule in §8 that §8 does not contain. DL §7: "a listener on written sees feed copies too, which is what makes feeds locally reactive, and the anti-loop rule in §8 is what keeps that from being a cycle." §8's feed pins are one-way, no write-back, receiver owns the copies. None of this prevents A→B→C→A.
  3. The invitation and consent surface is cited by two documents and specified by none. deploying.md §5 → "bootstrap.md §5 asks…"; bootstrap.md §8 → "§5's invitation and consent surface must carry the sentence"; bootstrap.md §5 is "What std.space.create must not do" and contains nothing of the kind.
  4. §8 says a guest is not a member; §3 says the platform mints them a members row. §8: "An ACL row may name a principal who is not a member of the space … the member just isn't from around here." §3, minting path (iv): "guest binding, when an inter-space share is accepted, a guest gets a members row with a null role." Reconcilable by reading "member" as "role-holder", which is how the corpus does it, but §8's wording is wrong as it stands, and R-G11a/GC092 depend on the §3 reading.
  5. Feeds and grants describe the same two-space act with two different vocabularies. security-logging-design.md §5 pins "a grant spanning two spaces is two grants, consented twice" and cites DL's own tier-2 bridge as the precedent. A feed is precisely that shape and is nevertheless specified as a single object with two consents and one revocation. Not a contradiction in outcome, but the two documents will diverge the moment either is implemented.
  6. Sealed fields are absent from the feed design entirely. Custody keys are per space, so every sealed field in a feed slice implies an indefinite standing key release in the sharing space. DL §7's release rule is written for a human caller invoking an op; §8's feed section does not mention sealed data, keys, or release. This is a gap rather than a conflict, but it is the gap that decides whether feeds are cheap or expensive.

Nothing above is settled. When a choice is made, the item that has to be written first is the invitation and consent surface, and the item that has to be ruled first is contradiction 1.
